Since the Bauhaus, German architecture has been a global export hit. Beginning in Germany, this style was carried, often unintentionally, across the world and became the foundation of international influences that still shape modern building culture today. This reputation is no myth: “Planned in Germany is a seal of quality,” as the Federal Chamber of German Architects once put it. But this seal is not a given — it depends on architecture still being created by architects in this country.

Frau Heese’s article touches a nerve because that is exactly what is at stake right now: preserving the allocation of public contracts is existential for our profession, and I am grateful that she has put this issue so clearly on the table. But her description operates on a level where established medium-sized firms and large offices are struggling over contracts, competitions and market share against turnkey providers. The problem goes deeper — one floor below, where newly founded offices like ours have to establish themselves at all before the question of procurement law even arises.

This lower level is the world of single- and two-family home new builds and renovations. This is where every generation of architects before us began — through attic conversions, bathroom renovations, small extensions. It is exactly in this field that crafts businesses and developers are most active today, offering all trades under one roof, employing or collaborating with their own architects, and growing into the turnkey firms of tomorrow.

That is the point missing from the procurement debate: the small businesses of today are becoming the major players of tomorrow — precisely through the market segment that should provide young offices with their first foothold. How are we supposed to reach the level where procurement plays a role if we are already denied work at this lower level?

We experience this in nearly every first conversation: homeowners contact us, ask for advice, and then decide in the end to go with the builder. Not because the idea there is better, but because they receive an immediate fixed price and do not have to worry about anything after placing the order. We, by contrast, can initially only quote a fee for our design services after the first planning phases — the costs of specialist planners and the exact construction costs are only known later. Anyone who seeks certainty first and quality second has often already made the decision before our offer even arrives.

This imbalance has a second, deeper layer. We study for five years and then spend years gaining practical experience, all to be allowed to use the protected title “architect.” Architects may engage in proper information advertising, but comparative advertising, superlatives, or promises of specific results are prohibited. This restraint is meant to protect the competitiveness of colleagues, and that is correct. A construction company that hires an architect and builds under its own name is not subject to this restriction, however, and can advertise with cut-rate prices without our professional code applying there.

For an established office, this is annoying. For a young office without references and without the reputation that would otherwise carry us, it is existentially threatening: we have nothing to counter with except quality itself — and that only becomes visible once the commission has already been awarded.

That is why it is not enough to defend procurement procedures. Before we even discuss tendering systems, the Chamber of Architects must look one floor lower: how do young offices get their first reference projects today, the projects that every later procurement depends on? And why does the restraint imposed on us by professional law not also apply to those who build under our title?

Only once these questions are answered does it make sense to fight to preserve procurement — otherwise we are defending a field that the next generation of architecture offices will never reach.
